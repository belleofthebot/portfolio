You cannot map a single novel. Travellers can find almost anything on Google Maps or Yelp — bars, museums, shopping. But readers who get to know places through books have no way to search for fictional landmarks. Where did Mrs. Dalloway start her walk? Where does Sherlock Holmes set up shop? The information exists, scattered across blogs and book tours, but there is no single place to find it. Travelling bookworms miss out on the joy of literature coming to life. How could we create an end to end app for readers visiting a new city to explore its literary spots? value proposition Bookspot brings literature to life with a map to beloved book settings — for book worms with a travel bug. 01 · empathize I started with readers, not screens I sketched a proto persona, then interviewed eight dedicated readers who love to travel. My script moved from reading habits to travel habits to the overlap between the two, closing on one abstract question: imagine your favourite book coming to life around you. Five interviews stood out. ￼ Laura, 32 engineer · Seattle e-books · literary fiction ￼ Natalie, 38 business owner · LA audiobooks · sci-fi & business ￼ Michelle, 28 teacher · Oakland physical books · non-fiction ￼ Nick, 41 game technician · Berkeley audiobooks · bios & fiction ￼ Brenda, 57 scholar & author · Newark all formats · comics & sci-fi what I heard, again and again ￼ Laura, 32 · engineer · literary fiction "I like that I can inhabit other worlds and realities, and experience the world through other people's visions." ￼ Michelle, 28 · teacher · non-fiction "Reading is such a great way to calm the mind and learn something new, a great opportunity to transport your mind somewhere else." ￼ Natalie, 38 · business owner · sci-fi "The stories that we tell influence our lives and our social structure and the way we interact with each other in the world." ￼ Brenda, 57 · scholar & author · everything "I love reading as the opportunity to experience new things, reading stuff that I could never have imagined on my own." Every conversation circled the same word without my prompting it. Affinity mapping and an empathy map confirmed the pattern: dedicated readers read to feel transported, and they choose travel destinations the same way. 02 · define One sentence to design against Julia, a dedicated reader, wants to visit her favourite book settings while travelling, to feel transported, curious, and joyful. Everything that made it into the app had to serve that sentence. Avoided: the long onboarding rigmarole. 04 · prototype Sketches, wireframes, and ten versions of one flow I will admit it: I fell into visual design early, exploring two polished directions before the wireframes were ready. Pulling back to paper sketches and lo-fi frames was the best decision of the project, the structure got solid before the paint went on. The feedback changed three big things. 1 · added a tutorial Testers wanted context before exploring, so a three step tour now opens the app, skippable, per Audible's lesson. 2 · added a list view Some testers wanted a longer scan of spots than the map allowed, so home gained a map and list toggle. 3 · deferred the asks Location and account requests moved deeper into the flow, users explore first, commit later. the design · 2026 Sage & parchment, four years wiser The bootcamp prototype earned its stamp, and the design kept evolving. This is Bookspot as I would ship it today: the same sage and parchment soul with a full bleed map, book covers as pins, chip filters, and collectible stamps. It is fully clickable — tap through onboarding, find a spot, get directions, collect your stamp. clickable prototype Bookspot, 2026 Tap through onboarding, find a spot, get directions, collect a stamp.
